Lab 1 · Describing lesions

Dr. Todd M. BellCLASS-C guide: Drs. Cassmann & Bell

A good description lets someone who was not at the necropsy picture the lesion. Lead with the organ and the distribution, then work through CLASS-C: Color, Location, Appearance, Size, Shape, Consistency.

  1. Hone your skills of observation and description.
  2. Apply these descriptive skills to lesions.
  3. Report checklist: organ → distribution → CLASS-C → morphologic diagnosis.

1

Distribution explorer

Distribution is often the most diagnostic part of a description, because the pattern tells you the route of injury. Pick a pattern, or drag the progression slider to watch multifocal lesions coalesce and become diffuse.

Source: How to Write a CLASS-C Description, pp. 3–5.

2

Distribution drill

Every pattern is generated fresh, so you can run this as long as you like. Use the logic flow: one spot? many spots? are they merging? is the whole organ affected?

3

Press test: consistency terms

Consistency is judged by gentle palpation and often gives the strongest hint about the process: fluid, cells or mineral. Press each specimen to see how the term behaves.

Source: How to Write a CLASS-C Description, p. 12.

4

Shape and contour gallery

Shape reflects how a lesion interacts with its surroundings, such as expanding outward or infiltrating inward. Study the profiles, then switch to quiz mode.

Source: How to Write a CLASS-C Description, p. 11.

5

CLASS-C tagger

Pick a category, then click each underlined phrase to tag it. Check your work when every phrase is tagged. The descriptions come from the CLASS-C guide and the Lab 2 and Lab 4 keys.

6

Description builder

Fill in the attributes for a specimen and the builder writes the description using the course formula: [Organ/Location] + [Distribution] + [CLASS-C attributes]. It flags anything you left out.

Lab 2 · Acute cell swelling

Dr. Todd M. BellZachary, PBVD 7th ed., pp. 22–30

Acute cell swelling is the first response of a cell to injury. Up to a point it is reversible. Past that point, the same cell becomes necrotic.

  1. Recognize gross and histologic lesions caused by cell swelling.
  2. Use the terminology for the gross and histologic changes of acute cell swelling.

Alive or dead?

Two reliable criteria tell you a cell is dead: loss of cell integrity, and nuclear change (absent, pyknotic, karyorrhectic or karyolytic nucleus). Classify each cell.

4

Same change, different names

Acute cell swelling looks the same under the microscope in different tissues, but the name depends on the cell type.

Necrosis vs. apoptosis

One slider drives both cells. Necrosis swells and bursts and draws inflammation; apoptosis shrinks and fragments into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies.

Source: Zachary, PBVD 7th ed., Figs. 1.12 and 1.17.

Erosion vs. ulcer

Necrosis of an epithelial surface sloughs dead cells. Depth decides the term. Drag the depth slider through the gastric wall (gross case 3, horse stomach).

Lab 4 · Adaptations, accumulations, calcification

Dr. Eric CassmannZachary, PBVD 7th ed., pp. 36–55

Chronic, sublethal injury lets cells adapt, accumulate substances or become mineralized. Recognizing which one you are looking at starts with the CLASS-C description.

  1. Describe specimens with CLASS-C and tell lesions from normal variation.
  2. Recognize atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia and dysplasia.
  3. Link chronic injury (nutrition, workload, hormones) to each adaptation.
  4. Describe lipid and glycogen accumulations, gross and microscopic.
  5. Explain how accumulations affect cell health.
  6. Differentiate dystrophic from metastatic calcification.
  7. Identify exogenous and endogenous pigments.
